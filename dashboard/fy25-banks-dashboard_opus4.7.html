FY25 · Comparative Banking Review Vol. I · Sydney · 2026

The Big Four,
and the Other One.

A comparison of CBA, Westpac, NAB, ANZ and Macquarie across the metrics that decide who wins shareholder capital — return, margin, efficiency, and resilience — for the year just ended.

§ IROE
Return on Equity · Cash basis · FY25

The capital question: does it earn its keep?

Return on equity is the single number that tells you whether a bank is creating value above its cost of capital. Anything below ~10% in this rate environment is an underperformance.

Fig. 1 — Cash ROE (%) Cost of equity ≈ 10–11%

CBA continues to occupy a category of one. At 13.5% cash ROE it sits roughly four percentage points clear of ANZ and Westpac, and is still pulling away on the back of disciplined retail franchise economics, dominant proprietary mortgage flow, and the highest interest-earning capital base of the five.

NAB's 11.4% is steady but unspectacular — the result of investing through the cycle rather than harvesting. The new CEO's stated ambition is "leading peer" returns, which on these numbers means closing roughly 200 bps of distance with CBA.

The gap between CBA and the rest is no longer a cycle quirk — it has hardened into structure. — Editorial commentary

ANZ at 8.1% is the standout disappointment, dragged down by ~$1.1bn of significant items including the ASIC settlement and restructuring. On an underlying basis (9.6%) it is closer to Westpac, but still earning below its cost of capital.

Macquarie at 11.2% is doing this with a fundamentally different business mix — asset management, markets, and a smaller but faster-growing bank — and remains the only one of the five that is materially diversified outside Australia.

§ IINIM
Net Interest Margin · Group basis · FY25

The price of borrowed money, sold on.

NIM is the spread between what a bank pays for funding and what it earns on lending. After two years of compression from mortgage and deposit competition, FY25 marks the first sign of stabilisation.

Fig. 2 — Group NIM (%) · trajectory and YoY change Note: Macquarie excluded — non-comparable business mix

The headline story is dispersion. CBA's 2.08% NIM is now 13 basis points wider than NAB's, 14 wider than Westpac's, and a remarkable 53 wider than ANZ's — a chasm for what is supposedly a commoditised retail product.

Two structural factors are doing the work: CBA earns more on its capital and replicating portfolio than peers (a function of size and funding profile), and its proprietary mortgage flow at 66% means less margin leaks to broker commissions.

ANZ's 1.55% reflects its institutional-heavy mix — those loans are lower-risk and lower-margin, but they are also the segment most exposed to global pricing. The arrival of a new CEO and the "ANZ 2030" strategy is partly a bet that this mix can be re-weighted.

The mortgage war is over. The deposit war never really started — banks just kept paying. — Margin commentary
§ IIIC/I
Cost-to-Income Ratio · FY25

Discipline, or investment?

A higher ratio means more cents of every revenue dollar consumed by running the bank. It is also where strategic bets — technology, restructuring, frontline hiring — show up first as a headwind.

Fig. 3 — Cost-to-Income Ratio (%) · lower is better Big 4 average · ~48%

CBA at ~42% is, by any global standard, an exceptionally efficient retail bank — the product of two decades of branch consolidation, a unified core platform, and scale. It is the moat.

Westpac's 53% (and ANZ's matching figure) tell a different story: ongoing transformation programs (UNITE at Westpac, ANZ 2030) plus restructuring charges. Westpac's expenses grew 9% in FY25, of which $273m was restructuring under "Fit for Growth". Real efficiency gains are still ahead.

NAB at ~46% sits between the leader and the laggards — a respectable position, though FY25 saw investment spend step up from $1.6bn to $1.8bn, much of it directed at frontline bankers and AI. The market reads this as productive investment.

Macquarie's ~71% looks alarming until you remember its income mix is dominated by asset management performance fees and markets — businesses that carry far higher variable comp ratios than retail banking. Compared peer-to-peer (BFS division), Macquarie is competitive.

§ IVCET1
Common Equity Tier 1 Capital Ratio · FY25

The capital that has nowhere to go.

CET1 is the highest-quality regulatory capital. APRA's "unquestionably strong" benchmark sits at 10.25% (after countercyclical buffer). All five are comfortably above. Excess capital is the new normal.

Fig. 4 — CET1 Ratio (%) vs APRA minimum APRA "unquestionably strong" · 10.25%

Every Australian bank is now running with capital well above what regulators demand and arguably what investors need. Macquarie's 12.8% is the highest of the five, in part because of how its non-bank businesses are structured (the harmonised global ratio is 17.6%).

CBA at 12.3% has been actively returning capital — buybacks plus dividend — but earnings keep refilling the buffer. The bank's stated operating target is >11.0% post-dividend, which leaves several billion dollars of explicit excess.

Australian banks have a high-class problem: too much capital, and a regulator unenthusiastic about giving it back. — Capital commentary

NAB at 11.7% sits lowest, but APRA's planned 2027 phase-out of AT1 capital (replaced by 0.25% CET1 + 1.25% Tier 2) means optical pressure on this number is structural rather than a sign of weakness. The pro-forma post-MLC sale is 11.81%.

§ VALL
Composite View · All metrics, FY25

A single picture, five different shapes.

Plotted on a common axis, the silhouettes of these banks reveal more than any individual league table. Tap a name to isolate.

Fig. 5 — Normalised performance shape · all metrics inverted so that "outward" = better Click a bank to highlight

Read the shapes, not the scores. CBA's silhouette stretches outward on every axis except CET1 — an unusually balanced profile. The clear conclusion: it is not winning on one metric, it is winning on all of them.

Macquarie's shape is asymmetric — strong on capital and ROE, weak on cost-to-income, and absent on NIM (a function of business model, not performance). It is a different animal entirely.

The tightest cluster is Westpac and ANZ: similar margins, similar efficiency, similar returns. Both are mid-cycle in their transformations, both are betting that FY26 and FY27 are when efficiency gains start landing.

NAB is in transition — the shape is unfinished. New CEO, refreshed strategy, stepped-up investment. The next two annual reports will determine which way it bends.

A note on comparability

Reporting periods differ: CBA reports to 30 June, Macquarie to 31 March, and ANZ, NAB and Westpac to 30 September. All figures are FY25 results as published in each bank's full-year announcement.

ROE for the Big Four is on a cash earnings basis. Macquarie reports statutory net profit. ANZ figures shown are on a cash basis including significant items; underlying figures (excluding ~$1.1bn of significant items) are noted in commentary where material.

Macquarie NIM is excluded from the comparison: it operates a fundamentally different business mix dominated by asset management, markets and capital activities. Cost-to-income is shown for completeness but is similarly affected.
